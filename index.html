A first-card guide for young adults

A credit card is a loan you take out every time you swipe.

Used well, it builds your credit score and pays you cash back. Used carelessly, it charges around 25% interest. Here is how to pick one and how to use it so the bank never earns a cent from you.

Part 1

Choosing a credit card

Every card offer has to list its rates and fees in a standard table, often called the Schumer box. Read that box before the marketing. These four numbers matter most.

APR

Annual Percentage Rate

The yearly interest charged on any balance you don't pay off by the due date. Most student cards are "variable," meaning the rate moves with the prime rate. If you pay your full statement balance every month, the APR never touches you.

Ask: what's the range, and is there a 0% intro period?

$0

Annual fee

A yearly charge just for having the card. For a first card, aim for $0. Also check the late fee, foreign transaction fee (if you'll travel), and cash advance fee.

Ask: would the rewards I earn actually beat this fee?

1–5%

Rewards

Cash back, points, or miles on what you spend. Flat-rate cards pay the same on everything. Rotating-category cards pay more on certain purchases but need you to sign up each quarter. Rewards are never worth paying interest for.

Ask: does this match where I actually spend money?

$500+

Credit limit

The most you can owe at once. First cards often start between $300 and $1,000. Your utilization (balance ÷ limit) is a big part of your score, so try to keep it under 30%, and ideally under 10%.

Ask: will my normal monthly spending stay under 30% of this?

Part 3

Six smart credit habits

  1. Pay the full statement balance

    Paying in full by the due date means you pay $0 interest, thanks to the grace period. Paying only the minimum is how $600 turns into $840.

  2. Turn on autopay

    Set autopay for at least the minimum so you never miss a due date. Payment history is the largest factor in your FICO score, and one 30-day late mark can stay on your report for seven years.

  3. Keep utilization under 30%

    On a $1,000 limit, that's a balance under $300 when your statement closes. Lower is better for your score.

  4. Only charge what's already in your account

    Treat the card like a debit card. If you couldn't pay cash for it today, don't put it on credit.

  5. Skip cash advances

    Cash advances usually charge a fee up front, a higher APR (around 28% on these cards), and interest starts immediately with no grace period.

  6. Check statements and your credit report

    Review charges every month to catch fraud, and pull your free reports at AnnualCreditReport.com to make sure everything is accurate.

  1. Best option: save first. Put $100 a month aside for six months and buy it with cash. You pay $0 interest and your score is untouched.
  2. If you need it now, use a 0% intro APR card and set up an automatic $100 payment. Six payments clear the $600 just before the promo ends. Write down the end date, since any leftover balance starts collecting interest at the regular rate.
  3. On a regular card, $100 a month still works, but it costs extra interest and takes an extra month. Don't add new charges while you pay it down, or your balance will climb past 60%.
  4. Never settle for the minimum payment. It roughly triples the time and adds hundreds in interest.

Why: the card can technically handle $600, but the real costs are interest and a temporary hit to your credit score. A plan that pays it off before interest builds up protects both.
