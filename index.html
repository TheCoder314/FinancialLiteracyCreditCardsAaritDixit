A first-card guide for young adults

Every credit card purchase is a short-term loan.

A credit card can help a young adult build a credit history and earn cash back. It can also become expensive, since most student cards charge around 25% interest on unpaid balances. This guide covers how to choose a first card and how to use it responsibly.

Choosing a Credit Card

Card issuers are required to list rates and fees in a standard table called the Schumer box. When comparing cards, four factors are the most important.

APR

Annual Percentage Rate

APR is the yearly interest rate charged on any balance left unpaid after the due date. Most student cards have a variable APR, which changes along with the prime rate. A cardholder who pays the full statement balance every month never pays this interest.

$0

Annual fee

Some cards charge a yearly fee just to keep the account open. Most good first cards have no annual fee. Other fees to check include late fees, foreign transaction fees, and cash advance fees.

1–5%

Rewards

Many cards give back cash, points, or miles on purchases. Flat-rate cards pay the same rate on everything. Rotating-category cards pay more on certain types of purchases, but the cardholder has to activate the categories each quarter. Interest charges quickly cancel out any rewards.

$500+

Credit limit

The credit limit is the maximum balance allowed on the card. First cards often start between $300 and $1,000. Credit utilization (balance divided by limit) has a large effect on credit scores, so it is best kept under 30%, and ideally under 10%.

Smart Credit Habits

  1. Pay the full statement balance

    Paying the full balance by the due date avoids interest completely because of the grace period. Paying only the minimum on $600 can raise the total cost to about $840.

  2. Use autopay

    Setting autopay for at least the minimum payment prevents missed due dates. Payment history is the largest factor in a FICO score, and a single payment that is 30 days late can stay on a credit report for seven years.

  3. Keep utilization under 30%

    On a $1,000 limit, this means keeping the balance under $300 when the statement closes. A lower balance is better for the credit score.

  4. Only charge what can already be paid

    A good rule is to treat a credit card like a debit card. If a purchase could not be paid in cash today, it should not go on the card.

  5. Avoid cash advances

    Cash advances come with an upfront fee and a higher APR (around 28% on these cards). Interest also starts right away, with no grace period.

  6. Check statements and credit reports

    Reviewing charges each month helps catch fraud early. Free credit reports from AnnualCreditReport.com show whether everything on file is accurate.

  1. Best option: save first. Setting aside $100 a month for six months and paying cash means no interest and no effect on the credit score.
  2. If the item is needed now, a 0% intro APR card is the next best choice, with an automatic $100 monthly payment. Six payments cover the $600 just before the promotion ends. It is important to note the end date, because any remaining balance will start collecting interest at the regular rate.
  3. On a regular card, $100 a month still works, but it adds about $48 in interest and one extra month. New charges should be avoided until the balance is paid off, or utilization will climb even higher.
  4. Minimum payments should be avoided. They stretch the payoff to almost three years and add about $240 in interest.

Reasoning: The card has enough room for the $600 purchase, but the real costs are interest and a temporary drop in the credit score. A plan that clears the balance before interest builds up limits both.
